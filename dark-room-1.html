The Dark Room #1 – Arkipelag, Dyrygent & 24 City: Eden's Garden or Valhalla

Old stories, modern problems. A story about the collapse of time and the laughter of Saturn's children.

It is not common that a dear friend or a close person recommends a good book, a good movie, good music or good art in general. So I had to take the time to write this perspective on the recently watched Arkipelag, by Alex Schulman, the first feature film directed by the Swedish journalist.

At first glimpse, a really small one indeed, as I did not want to demotivate myself or find an excuse not to go to the movies, I decided not to read anything about it before watching it. So truly my expectations were non-existent, or to be frank, I always expect a bit of chaos, so my hopes were grounded on my beliefs: chaos and randomness. The movie starts with a playful energy, the kind normally carried by comedies, in a playful space such as "the school". The place where we all hope to go back, not to re-learn, but to repeat all of those actions in a world ignorant of consequences (or at least not too aware of them yet) and eternal nostalgia. But also the place of brutal relations and the systematization of violence, the place that teaches us the good and the bad, of course with the great support of families, which in modern times support it more and more ;). The movie inherits this ambiguity or duality that the school, specifically the classroom, represents, and shifts that comedy touch closer to tragedy, not losing the note close to the laugh, but making clearer and clearer that if any humor exists, it is probably the dark one, especially when the absurdity grows and grows. It did not need much more to seduce me, maybe because I manipulate myself so much that my basic instincts are easy to please; just a short conversation between the parents of the students and the teacher responsible for the class. Those 8 minutes, approximately, of elegance and obscenity, are refreshing in a time of political correctness that governs mainstream cinema. Luckily for all of us, most workers in schools and other industries are still not psychopaths, which makes the error of the teacher more and more believable. In her journey to ask the parents for support with their kids' education, due to a case of cyberbullying, the teacher was not careful enough erasing the footprints of the path to make it fully politically correct, which makes her, unluckily, fall into the opposite reaction: pointing fingers at the individuals involved in the problem.

It is that last act, the finger pointing, which converts the classroom into a battlefield where every parent defends their family or clan, and which changes the tone from Eden's garden, a place without consequences, full of innocence, pure, into a process, a divine process which does not depend on personal efforts, rather on the Valkyries, who will take some of us into Valhalla. The place where warriors resurrect every day to die again, a place where we fall, we resurrect, we celebrate, a place that reminds us who we are and what our destiny is, but only for those brave enough. Sorry, I lied: who are those?

Adult Supervision and Saturn

The movie answers, and the answer is nobody and everyone, and maybe that answer is rather a debate that depends on our perspective. Three fathers join their daughters on a school trip to an island, adult supervision, as the English title of the movie suggests, and sorry, I lied again, there is not a single adult there. It only takes a few more minutes after the classroom battle to see that the fathers on the boat, carriers of this tragicomedy, when the lights are not on them, or in other words, when they are hidden from the expectations of society, turn out to be immature, maybe even more immature than their own kids. At the end of this scene, the class and the fathers arrive at the island, a closed space, with no one to call and no institution to hide behind, so friction starts accelerating. What the fathers buried, and what we started to see in that simple interaction on the boat, comes back to the surface, not as a ghost that will terrify the kids, but as behaviors, fights between them or, even worse, through the kids. The ones who came to protect are the ones who devour.

The Romans called him Saturn, the Greeks Kronos, and they already confused him with Chronos, time, or maybe they just unified two living ideas. Nevertheless, here both are present, the father and time, both represented as the destruction of memory, the destruction of an uncertain present in favor of a controlled future. The father who ate his children so that none of them would take his place, the father who wants to keep the hierarchy solid, eternal. Coming back to the movie, Arkipelag is a story of Saturn's children who became Saturn, just as Zeus did: fathers feeding their daughters with their own past traumas, the ones they never digested. And this is where we should introduce the first lie, the lie of fear. The teacher trying to erase the footprints, the parents defending the image of the innocent family, both protect the same thing, their own image. The form of this lie relies on aesthetics, a mask that pretends to be life, and its goal is to collapse time: to live in a present without a past and its consequences, an Eden with no consequences. But the past does not collapse, it waits, and on an island it has nowhere else to go. When I realized it, I could only laugh ;), I do not remember if I was the only one.

So, inspired, and as any good Saturn kid, without the supervision of my parents, instead of facing my own island, I went to hide in the archive, where my dear lover encouraged me to go when needed, my cave: the cinematheque in Stockholm, next to my new "home". A Valhalla of its own, where old films fall every night and rise again on the screen. I went to look for the brave among the dead, the only place where one might be able to find them, and I found a Polish conductor from 1980 and a Chinese factory from 2008. The same Saturn, Father Time, eating in two other languages.

In Dyrygent, Andrzej Wajda brings Jan Lasocki, an old and famous conductor, back to the Polish town where he was young, and the local conductor, Adam, sees in him the one who will take his place. Adam is Saturn with a key: he protects his hierarchical position with the institution, like a father keeps his power with the family, with the rules, with the gesture of a conductor that has no music behind it, or who forgot how to play it, if he ever knew how to. A different lie of fear, not about who we are but about what we can do, and as happens with every lie of this kind, the past comes back to expose it, here in the form of the music itself, the one thing you cannot fake in front of an orchestra. Between them stands Marta, Adam's wife, a violinist. She does not overthrow anyone, she does not play Zeus, she just stops holding the lie and opens the door outward. A limit, not a rebellion: the room, not the pond.

In 24 City, Jia Zhangke films Factory 420 ;), a military factory in Chengdu, before it is demolished to build a luxury complex called, precisely, 24 City. The workers speak one after another in front of the camera, a Greek chorus of the defeated, the Trojans looking at their city before the fall, with rawness, without smiles, just a full shot of the workers and their families, with tears for a lost heritage. However, the movie relies on an honest trick and probably the only reasonable lie, a lie that we need, not for us but for the others. Jia lies, and he lets you know that he lies (not within the movie but in its production), and with that lie he saves what the demolition wanted to erase, history and its builders. Like Rhea wrapping a stone as if it were a child, Jia wraps a movie as if it were a documentary. Does that make us Saturn, swallowing the stone?

The interviews move from the old generation to the new, and with them the friction changes form. The older workers speak of their bodies, their work, the moves across the country, what they lost and what they learned. Their work grounded them. They remember the teachers who showed them how to become artisans, how to meet the material friction of reality with their hands. They wanted to prosper, to find a passion or let it find them, though, to be fair, they did enjoy those blue factory uniforms. Even that prestige can only partly seduce the young. Su Na, the daughter of workers, has no interest in the factory; she buys clothes for the rich in Hong Kong. Yet she promises to buy her parents an apartment in 24 City, the complex built on the ruins of the factory where they worked. This is not indifference, nor quite rebellion. It is closer to revolution in its old sense, revolvere, to turn: the family remains in orbit, but care takes a different form. Her parents protected her through work that fed the family; she wants to protect them with a beautiful apartment. The factory was a house with one room, its workers sharing work, food and sleep. 24 City is a house full of locked rooms, and Su Na wants to give her parents one of their own. It will be a room if memory can flow through it, a pond if memory stays buried beneath its foundations. Is this a revolution, or the system recovering itself, care paid in the currency of aesthetics?

Maybe the question is badly asked, and the problem might be the word itself. Aesthetics comes from the Greek aisthēsis: sensation, perception, the ability to feel; it was only in the 18th century that it became connected with the theory of beauty and art. Its opposite is anesthesia, not feeling, and what we sell today under the name of aesthetics is, most of the time, the latter: a beautiful way of not feeling the crack, of not feeling reality and the consequences of our decisions. The difference is not the object, a uniform, an apartment or a film, but what it does to us. The anesthetic leaves us the same and asking for more of what we already were, demanding, thinking our needs are obligations, basically a transformation of the image with no substantial change; the aesthetic, on the other hand, leaves us changed, producing a transition that needs the other, and it hurts, a little or a lot, that we still do not know.

Now, looking back, the three films tell the same story at three different scales, the father, the master and the state, the same Saturn in three sizes, just like turbulence, a multi-scale problem. Each one lies about something different, which deep down is the fear of being caught as an impostor. The family in Arkipelag lies about who we are, the innocent clan or tribe; the institution in Dyrygent lies about what we can do, the podium without music; the state in 24 City lies about where we are going, a progress built on ruins. Three lies born from fear, three anesthetics: a mask, a gesture, a uniform turned into an apartment. All of them built to cover a crack, a void that feeds on our insecurities and fears of the unknown, a suffering that we still cannot heal, collapsing time to live in a present that owes nothing to the past and to those who helped us along the way. And as usual, what is broken remains broken, so the crack comes back in all three films: on the island, in the music, in the faces of the workers. Against those lies, three answers: a laugh, a limit, and an honest lie.

So, who are those, the brave ones? Not the ones who go back to Eden, as Eden is guarded by a cherub with a flaming sword, and going back to childhood when one already knows about its lack of innocence is regression, just ask the fathers on the boat ;). Not the ones who win Valhalla either, a battle repeated every day until the final defeat. And not Zeus, as he is the clearest revolution, in that old sense of the word, revolvere, a full turn that comes back to where it started. He escaped Saturn thanks to his mother, grew up hidden, came back, made his father vomit his brothers and took his place, only to later swallow his pregnant wife out of fear. The wheel turned, and Saturn was back, only with a different face on the throne. That is why the question about Su Na stays open: her revolution can still be the one we just described, the system turning around to find itself again, or something else, a regeneration, re-generare, to beget again, a new generation born from the old one without devouring it, keeping the scar.

What remains is memory, our only temple, the true one, not the one we build on top of it to collapse it: the apartment, the podium, the innocent family. The Vikings would say the temple is the good name, as in the Hávamál: cattle die, kinsmen die, you yourself die, but the good name of the one who earned it never dies, a name too often earned by killing others. The temple I mean has no names: it is the workers of Factory 420 that nobody will remember as heroes, their memory reincarnated in the bodies of actresses so that it would not disappear under the foundations of a new future, a new building. Maybe this is the answer to my question: the brave ones are not those who die to be remembered, but those who stay with what is broken without covering it: a wife who opens the door, a director who confesses his lie, workers without names, a son who forgives his father or mother, a boyfriend who says sorry. Nobody and everyone, as I said, and sorry, this time I did not lie.
